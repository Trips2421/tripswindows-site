Skylight Cleaning in Los Angeles: A Homeowner's Guide

2026-06-22 · 7 min read

Skylights are the one piece of glass in the house almost nobody thinks to clean — until the room they light starts feeling dim and the glass overhead looks milky against the sky. Because they face straight up and usually sit out of easy reach, skylights collect more grime than any window in the home and get attention the least. This guide covers why LA skylights get dirty so fast, how often they really need cleaning, why the exterior side is genuinely risky to reach, and what a professional visit involves.

Why do skylights get dirty faster than regular windows?

Skylights get dirty faster than vertical windows because they face straight up, so gravity works against them. Every airborne particle that settles out of the sky — dust, pollen, marine-layer salt, soot, and bird droppings — lands directly on the glass and stays there. A vertical window sheds most of that; a skylight catches all of it.

Rain doesn't rinse a skylight clean the way people assume, either. It leaves mineral spots as it evaporates, and on low-slope units water pools and dries into rings. Add tree sap and leaf litter from overhead canopy and the glass can look hazy within a few months even when the rest of the home's windows still look fine. It's the same mineral-deposit mechanism behind streaky windows after a quick wipe-down — just concentrated on a single upward-facing pane.

How often should I have my skylights cleaned in Los Angeles?

Most Los Angeles homes do well with skylight cleaning twice a year — typically late spring and early fall. That cadence catches the spring pollen load and resets the glass before the dry, dusty stretch of summer and fall.

Some homes need more. Properties under heavy tree canopy, near the coast in Venice or Santa Monica where marine-layer salt builds up, or in dusty hillside and canyon areas like the Hollywood Hills benefit from quarterly attention. If a skylight sits directly under a bird-perching branch, droppings are acidic enough to etch the glass over time, so those get cleaned more often. The simplest fix for the "I forgot about the skylights" problem is to put them on the same schedule as your regular window cleaning.

Can I clean my skylights myself?

The interior side, often yes. If you can reach it safely with an extension pole, a microfiber cloth and a streak-free glass solution will handle the inside surface of most accessible skylights.

The exterior is a different matter. It usually means getting onto the roof, which is the single most dangerous part of skylight maintenance. Walking a pitched or tile roof, working over a glass surface, and reaching units near the roof edge are exactly how DIY injuries happen. On a single-story home with a low, walkable roof and a skylight set well in from the edge, a careful homeowner can manage it. On two-story homes, steep pitches, or tile, the math tips firmly toward professional service.

Is it safe to walk on the roof to clean a skylight?

It carries real risk and should be approached carefully. Tile and slate roofs crack under foot traffic, asphalt shingles get slick when damp, and the area around a skylight is often the most fragile part of the roof.

The most important rule: never step or kneel on the skylight glass itself. Many skylights are not rated to hold a person's weight and can break through — a fall hazard that has caused serious injuries. Professionals work around this with roof-edge anchors, harnesses, weight-distributing pads, and water-fed poles that let them clean from a stable position rather than standing over the glass. This is the same reasoning behind why DIY high-window cleaning usually isn't worth the risk.

What's the best way to clean skylight glass without streaks?

Rinse off the loose grit first, wash with a mild glass solution and a soft sponge or microfiber, then squeegee or dry with a clean microfiber cloth before the water evaporates. The sequence matters — dragging a squeegee across gritty glass scratches it.

A few things to avoid: don't clean in direct midday sun, which dries the solution too fast and leaves streaks; skip abrasive pads and ammonia-heavy products on coated or plastic skylights; and never use a pressure washer, which can force water past the seals and cause leaks. Dome and tubular skylights are often acrylic, not glass — those need a plastic-safe cleaner, since standard glass cleaner can cloud the surface permanently.

My skylight is leaking or stained — is that a cleaning problem?

Not always, and this is an important distinction. A stain or water mark around the inside frame is often a seal or flashing issue rather than dirt, and cleaning won't fix it. Cleaning addresses haze, mineral spots, pollen, and surface debris.

If you see water intrusion, condensation trapped between panes, or staining on the drywall around the skylight, that points to a sealing or roofing repair — a separate job from cleaning. A thorough cleaning visit will flag these conditions so you can get ahead of them, but it's worth knowing the difference before you book, so your expectations match what cleaning can actually do.

Does cleaning skylights actually make the room brighter?

Yes — noticeably. A skylight is usually the brightest light source in the room it serves, and a film of dust and mineral haze can cut the light it transmits significantly. Because the buildup happens gradually over months, homeowners rarely notice the room dimming; they just notice how much warmer and brighter a kitchen, stairwell, or bathroom feels right after the skylight is cleaned.

There's an aesthetic payoff too. Clean skylight glass reads as clear rather than milky from inside, which matters a lot for the look of vaulted ceilings and open living spaces where the skylight is part of the architecture.

Do you clean tubular skylights and sun tunnels too?

Yes. Tubular skylights (sun tunnels) collect dust on the exterior dome and, over years, on the interior diffuser at the ceiling. The exterior dome is cleaned like any rooftop unit; the interior diffuser can usually be removed and wiped.

Flat-glass skylights, dome skylights, ridge and pyramid units, and operable (venting) skylights each get a slightly different approach, but all are part of routine residential service. Operable skylights also benefit from clearing debris out of the track and weep channels so they keep sealing and opening properly — the same kind of detail work that keeps window tracks and screens functioning.

Should I clean skylights at the same time as my windows?

Bundling skylight cleaning with your regular window cleaning is the most efficient approach. The crew is already on site with ladders, poles, and roof equipment, so adding skylights avoids a second trip and a second setup. It also keeps everything on one consistent schedule — which solves the real reason skylights get neglected: out of sight, out of mind.

If you're already booking a visit, it's worth thinking about the whole exterior at once. Adding gutter cleaning and a pressure-washing pass in the same appointment gives the home a full reset, with the work sequenced so nothing freshly cleaned gets re-soiled.
