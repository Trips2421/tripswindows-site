Soft Washing vs Pressure Washing: Which Does Your LA Home Need?

2026-06-25 · 8 min read

Most homeowners use "pressure washing" as a catch-all term for cleaning the outside of a house. But there are actually two different methods — pressure washing and soft washing — and using the wrong one on the wrong surface is one of the most common ways people accidentally damage their own homes. This guide explains the difference, which surfaces need which method, and how to tell whether your property needs one, the other, or both.

What is the difference between soft washing and pressure washing?

Pressure washing uses a high-pressure stream of water (typically 1,300 to 4,000 PSI) to physically blast dirt, grime, and stains off hard, durable surfaces like concrete and brick. Soft washing uses very low pressure (around 100 PSI or less, similar to a garden hose) combined with specialized biodegradable cleaning solutions that break down dirt, algae, mold, and mildew chemically rather than by force.

The simplest way to think about it: pressure washing cleans with force, soft washing cleans with chemistry. Both have their place. The right choice depends entirely on the surface and on what's actually growing or stuck on it — and a good exterior washing service carries the equipment to do both in a single visit.

Which surfaces need soft washing instead of pressure washing?

Soft washing is the correct method for any surface that high pressure could damage: roof shingles and tiles, stucco and EIFS, painted wood siding, vinyl siding, screens, outdoor furniture, and anything with organic growth like algae or mildew that needs to be killed rather than just rinsed off.

On these surfaces, high-pressure water can crack stucco, force water behind siding, strip paint, or dislodge roof granules. The low pressure of soft washing cleans them just as thoroughly without the risk — and because it treats organic growth at the root, the results last longer.

Which surfaces should be pressure washed?

Pressure washing is best for hard, non-porous, durable surfaces that can take the force: concrete driveways, sidewalks, patios, brick, pavers, stone hardscape, and concrete block walls. These surfaces hold up to high pressure and often need it to remove embedded oil stains, tire marks, mineral deposits, and ground-in grime that a low-pressure rinse simply can't lift.

This is the work most people picture when they think about pressure washing — and it's genuinely the right tool for the job on hardscape. Our guide on how often to pressure wash your driveway covers the cadence for the most common pressure-washed surface in LA.

Can pressure washing damage my house?

Yes — pressure washing the wrong surface is one of the most common causes of avoidable exterior home damage. High pressure can crack and chip stucco, force water behind siding and into wall cavities (leading to mold and rot), strip paint, etch wood, blast mortar out from between bricks, shatter old single-pane windows, and strip the protective granules off asphalt roof shingles.

This is exactly why professionals match the method to the surface rather than using maximum pressure on everything. A lot of the "pressure washing damage" homeowners discover — gouged wood, pitted stucco, paint peeling in sheets — comes from a well-meaning DIY afternoon or an inexperienced crew running one setting on every surface.

Is soft washing better than pressure washing?

Neither is universally better — they solve different problems. Soft washing is better for delicate surfaces and for killing organic growth like algae, moss, and mildew, because the cleaning solution treats the growth at the root so it stays cleaner longer. Pressure washing is better for hard surfaces with embedded grime, oil, and mineral stains that need mechanical force to remove.

A quality exterior cleaning uses both: soft washing for the house, roof, and delicate areas, and pressure washing for the driveway, patio, and hardscape. The skill is knowing where one ends and the other begins.

How long does soft washing keep surfaces clean compared to pressure washing?

Soft washing typically keeps surfaces clean longer when the dirt is organic. Because the cleaning solution kills algae, mold, and mildew spores rather than just rinsing the visible layer away, regrowth is much slower — results often last several times longer than a plain pressure rinse on the same surface.

Pressure washing removes what's there in the moment but doesn't treat biological growth, so algae and mildew can return faster. For LA's marine-influenced and shaded areas — think north-facing walls in Venice or Santa Monica, or homes under heavy tree canopy in Brentwood — soft washing's longevity is a real advantage.

Do I need soft washing or pressure washing for my roof?

Roofs should almost always be soft washed, never pressure washed. High-pressure water strips the protective granules off asphalt shingles (shortening roof life), can crack or dislodge tiles, and forces water under the roofing where it causes leaks — and it typically voids the roofing manufacturer's warranty.

Soft washing safely removes the black streaks (a type of algae called Gloeocapsa magma), moss, and lichen common on shaded LA roofs without any of that risk. If a company offers to pressure wash your roof, treat it as a red flag — it's one of the questions worth asking before you hire, which we cover in how to choose a window cleaning company in LA.

Does soft washing use harsh chemicals?

Professional soft washing uses cleaning solutions diluted to safe, effective concentrations and rinsed thoroughly afterward. Reputable companies use biodegradable detergents and protect surrounding landscaping by pre-wetting plants and rinsing them after the wash.

The solutions are designed to break down dirt and kill organic growth, then neutralize — not to linger. A professional matches solution strength to the surface and the growth, which is safer and more predictable than the trial-and-error mixing many DIYers attempt with store-bought bleach.

Can I rent a pressure washer and do this myself?

You can pressure wash a concrete driveway or patio yourself with a rented or consumer-grade machine, and for hard, ground-level hardscape that's often reasonable. The problems start when homeowners point that same machine at stucco, siding, roofs, or windows — the surfaces that need soft washing, not pressure.

DIY soft washing is harder because it requires the right solution, dilution, and application equipment, plus ladder safety for roofs and second stories. For house exteriors, roofs, and multi-story work, professional service avoids the most expensive mistakes — the same logic behind why DIY window cleaning often isn't worth it.

Why do LA homes get algae, mildew, and black streaks?

Coastal and shaded LA homes are prone to algae, mildew, and black roof streaks because of marine-layer moisture, morning fog, and the mild climate that lets organic growth thrive year-round. North-facing walls, shaded eaves, areas under tree canopy, and surfaces near sprinklers stay damp longest and grow the most.

This kind of organic growth is exactly what soft washing is designed to treat. The same marine moisture that drives this growth is what makes coastal glass haze over — we break that mechanism down in how June Gloom marine layer affects LA windows.

How often should I have my home's exterior washed in Los Angeles?

Most LA homes benefit from exterior washing once or twice a year, with the cadence depending on exposure. Driveways and high-traffic hardscape often need pressure washing once or twice a year; house exteriors and roofs usually do well with annual soft washing, more often for homes under heavy tree canopy, near the coast, or in shaded, damp microclimates.
